The native signals produced by an environment’s pressure, drift, and structural geometry.

Purpose
Environmental Signals describe the ambient signal field generated by a location itself.
They are not emitted by species, roles, or artifacts — they arise from:
• 	pressure gradients
• 	drift vectors
• 	environmental geometry
• 	lineage‑active terrain
• 	collapse scars
• 	distortion fields
Environmental Signals define how a place communicates.
They shape:
• 	perception
• 	movement
• 	identity behavior
• 	species activity
• 	collapse likelihood
• 	recovery patterns

The Three Classes of Environmental Signals
Environmental Signals fall into three canonical classes:

1. Baseline Signals
The natural, stable signals of an environment in resonance.
• 	predictable
• 	low‑noise
• 	tied to environmental identity
• 	support stable movement and perception
Examples:
• 	Branch hum
• 	Shore rhythm
• 	Deep resonance
• 	Mist whisper
Function:
Provide orientation, stability, and environmental coherence.

2. Drift Signals
Signals generated by movement, flow, or vector alignment within the environment.
• 	directional
• 	dynamic
• 	pressure‑responsive
• 	tied to drift geometry
Examples:
• 	Cross‑current pulse
• 	Spiral drift tone
• 	Updraft shimmer
• 	Undertow call
Function:
Guide movement, indicate flow, and reveal drift stability or instability.

3. Distortion Signals
Signals produced when the environment is under stress, collapse, or pressure imbalance.
• 	warped
• 	noisy
• 	pattern‑breaking
• 	often unpredictable
Examples:
• 	Echo‑curved return
• 	Mirage shimmer
• 	Collapse spiral
• 	Boundary‑thin static
Function:
Warn of instability, reveal collapse zones, and indicate environmental danger.

Environmental Signal Behavior
Environmental Signals behave according to three principles:

1. Pressure Modulation
Signal intensity rises or falls with environmental pressure.
• 	high pressure → sharper, louder, more urgent
• 	low pressure → softer, diffuse, stable

2. Drift Coupling
Signals attach to drift vectors.
• 	stable drift → clean directional signals
• 	unstable drift → warped or spiraling signals

3. Boundary Reflection
Signals reflect off environmental boundaries.
• 	tight boundaries → strong echoes
• 	porous boundaries → signal bleed
• 	collapsed boundaries → signal inversion

Environmental Signal States
Environments cycle through three signal states:

1. Resonant State
Baseline signals dominate.
• 	stable
• 	predictable
• 	low distortion

2. Transitional State
Drift signals dominate.
• 	dynamic
• 	shifting
• 	pressure‑responsive

3. Distorted State
Distortion signals dominate.
• 	noisy
• 	warped
• 	collapse‑prone

Environmental Signal Failure Modes
Environmental Signals fail when:
• 	pressure spikes
• 	drift collapses
• 	boundaries rupture
• 	distortion fields intensify
• 	collapse wells form
Failure produces:
• 	echo loops
• 	spiral wells
• 	mirage overlays
• 	signal inversion
• 	collapse drift

Stabilization & Recovery
Environmental Signals stabilize through:
• 	Pressure Return
• 	Drift Return
• 	Signal Return
• 	Boundary Reformation
• 	Environmental Reorientation
These processes restore the environment to its baseline signal state.
