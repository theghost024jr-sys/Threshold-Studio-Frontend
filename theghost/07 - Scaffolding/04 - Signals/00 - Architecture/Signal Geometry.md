The structural shape of a signal: its curvature, boundaries, vectors, and deformation behaviors.

Purpose
Signal Geometry defines the form and behavior of signals within Threshold.
It explains how signals:
• 	hold shape
• 	bend under pressure
• 	distort in unstable environments
• 	collapse under overload
• 	reflect off boundaries
• 	resonate with identity and lineage
• 	propagate through drift fields
Signal Geometry is the physics of meaning‑shape.

The Four Axes of Signal Geometry
Every signal has a four‑axis structure:

1. Harmonic Axis
The frequency and pattern of the signal.
Determines:
• 	clarity
• 	resonance
• 	distortion susceptibility
• 	lineage activation
Strong harmonic: clean, stable, interpretable
Weak harmonic: noisy, unstable, collapse‑prone

2. Drift Axis
The directional movement of the signal.
Determines:
• 	vector alignment
• 	speed
• 	curvature
• 	susceptibility to environmental drift
Stable drift: predictable transmission
Unstable drift: warped or spiraling transmission

3. Boundary Axis
How the signal interacts with edges and structures.
Determines:
• 	reflection
• 	bleed
• 	inversion
• 	containment
Strong boundaries: crisp reflections
Porous boundaries: signal bleed
Collapsed boundaries: inversion or echo‑loops

4. Pressure Axis
How the signal responds to environmental pressure.
Determines:
• 	amplitude
• 	intensity
• 	compression
• 	expansion
High pressure: sharper, more urgent signals
Low pressure: diffuse, soft, ambient signals

Signal Geometry Table


Curvature Types
Signals bend under pressure and drift in predictable ways:

1. Linear Curvature
Signal travels in a straight or gently bending line.
• 	stable
• 	predictable
• 	low distortion
Seen in resonant environments.

2. Radial Curvature
Signal expands or contracts outward/inward.
• 	pressure imbalance
• 	boundary influence
• 	environmental compression
Seen in pressure spikes or collapse zones.

3. Spiral Curvature
Signal twists around its drift axis.
• 	rotational distortion
• 	directional confusion
• 	collapse potential
Seen in distortion fields or unstable drift.

4. Echo Curvature
Signal loops back on itself.
• 	reflection overload
• 	boundary recursion
• 	harmonic instability
Seen in echo‑curved distortion zones.

Signal Geometry States
Signals exist in three canonical geometric states:

1. Coherent Geometry
All axes aligned, curvature minimal.
• 	clean harmonics
• 	stable drift
• 	predictable reflection
• 	balanced pressure
This is the geometry of signal clarity.

2. Distorted Geometry
One or more axes misaligned.
• 	warped drift
• 	harmonic instability
• 	boundary bleed
• 	pressure compression
This is the geometry of distortion.

3. Collapsed Geometry
Axes lose structural integrity.
• 	noise
• 	spirals
• 	inversion
• 	recursive loops
This is the geometry of signal collapse.

Geometry Transitions
Signals transition through predictable phases:
1. 	Coherent → Distorted
Drift or pressure destabilizes the geometry.
2. 	Distorted → Collapsed
Harmonics break or boundaries rupture.
3. 	Collapsed → Recovered
Signal Return clears noise and resets geometry.
4. 	Recovered → Resonant
Harmonics re‑align and drift stabilizes.

Environmental Influence on Signal Geometry
Signal Geometry is shaped by:
• 	drift vectors
• 	pressure gradients
• 	boundary strength
• 	distortion density
• 	collapse wells
• 	resonance pockets
Examples:
• 	Mist bends signals into spiral curvature
• 	Branch stabilizes linear curvature
• 	Deep compresses signals into radial curvature
• 	Shore oscillates curvature between linear and wave‑patterned

Identity Influence on Signal Geometry
Identity Geometry determines:
• 	how signals are interpreted
• 	how boundaries reflect or absorb signals
• 	how lineage threads activate
• 	how distortion is resisted or amplified
Identity and signal are co‑geometric.
