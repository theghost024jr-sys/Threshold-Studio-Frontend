Purpose
Signal Interpretation defines how signals are read, decoded, and understood across all layers of Threshold.
A signal is not the event.
A signal is the system’s reaction to the event.
Interpretation is the process of:
• 	identifying the signal class
• 	determining drift direction
• 	mapping collapse risk
• 	reading emotional or structural content
• 	translating the signal into actionable meaning
Signal Interpretation is the translation engine of the entire system.

1. What a Signal Is
A signal is any observable change in:
• 	behavior
• 	tone
• 	pattern
• 	structure
• 	emotional field
• 	relational stance
• 	environmental response
Signals are outputs, not causes.

2. Signal Interpretation Framework
Signal Interpretation uses a three‑step process:
2.1 Classification
Determine the signal’s type:
• 	Soft Signal (early drift)
• 	Sharp Signal (active destabilization)
• 	Hard Signal (collapse activation)
See: [[Collapse Signals]]
2.2 Contextualization
Interpret the signal through:
• 	Role
• 	Identity
• 	Species
• 	Environment
• 	Drift state
• 	Overlay influence
Signals cannot be interpreted in isolation.
2.3 Direction Mapping
Determine what the signal is pointing toward:
• 	drift direction
• 	collapse direction
• 	recovery direction
• 	relational need
• 	structural mismatch
Signals always point somewhere.

3. Signal Types and Their Meaning
3.1 Emotional Signals
Soft, relational, intuitive.
Examples:
• 	tone shifts
• 	hesitation
• 	emotional fog
• 	boundary blur
Meaning:
• 	early drift
• 	emotional overload
• 	relational mismatch

3.2 Behavioral Signals
Observable actions or patterns.
Examples:
• 	withdrawal
• 	scanning
• 	over‑functioning
• 	rigidity
Meaning:
• 	mid‑stage drift
• 	role misalignment
• 	environmental pressure

3.3 Structural Signals
System‑level changes.
Examples:
• 	shutdown
• 	lock
• 	dissociation
• 	pattern enforcement
Meaning:
• 	collapse activation
• 	identity fracture
• 	structural overload

4. Interpretation by Layer
4.1 Role Layer
Signals reveal:
• 	drift direction
• 	role misalignment
• 	collapse risk
• 	behavioral loop activation
Example:
Sentinel scanning → early drift toward Judge.

4.2 Identity Layer
Signals reveal:
• 	self‑model instability
• 	narrative contradiction
• 	emotional inversion
• 	identity drift
Example:
Tone flattening → identity soft signal.

4.3 Species Layer
Signals reveal:
• 	instinctive bias
• 	collapse style
• 	relational orientation
Example:
Prey withdrawal → soft collapse signal.

4.4 Environmental Layer
Signals reveal:
• 	mismatch
• 	overload
• 	pressure gradients
Example:
Increased vigilance → environmental threat saturation.

5. Drift Interpretation
Signals map directly onto drift mechanics:
• 	Soft Signals → Early Drift
• 	Sharp Signals → Active Drift
• 	Hard Signals → Collapse
Interpretation determines:
• 	drift speed
• 	drift severity
• 	drift direction
• 	drift reversibility
See: [[Role Drift]] · [[Identity Drift]]

6. Collapse Interpretation
Collapse signals must be interpreted through:
• 	collapse type
• 	collapse direction
• 	collapse propagation risk
• 	collapse recovery path
Collapse is not failure — it is a structural reset.
See: [[Collapse Signals]] · [[Role Collapse]]

7. Recovery Interpretation
Signals also indicate recovery potential:
• 	softening
• 	re‑grounding
• 	re‑alignment
• 	re‑coherence
Recovery signals are the inverse of collapse signals.
See: [[Recovery Signals]]

8. Interpretation Errors
Misinterpretation occurs when:
• 	signals are taken literally
• 	context is ignored
• 	species bias is unaccounted for
• 	role drift is unseen
• 	identity drift is mistaken for behavior
Interpretation requires contextual intelligence, not surface reading.
