Rava’s passive side is heavy in a different way — not tectonic, but **melancholic**. When erosion begins, he doesn’t rage; he slows. The plates of his body stop shifting, the glow in his cracks dims to a faint ember. He kneels beside the worn stone faces and runs a hand over the softened edges, feeling the years slip away under his touch.

He doesn’t fight the erosion — he knows it’s part of the cycle — but he mourns the memory that’s being lost. Each grain of sand is a syllable falling out of the language of stone. Each waterfall that deepens a canyon is a line erased from the planet’s oldest script.

In those moments, Rava becomes almost still enough to be mistaken for a mountain. Dust gathers on his shoulders. Moss begins to grow in the seams of his armor. The world forgets he can move.

And that’s his sadness: He loves the permanence of stone, but he lives in a universe that insists on change. He can hold the mountain together for a while, but not forever. So he watches the erosion, listens to the slow crumble, and whispers — not to stop it, but to **remember** it.
