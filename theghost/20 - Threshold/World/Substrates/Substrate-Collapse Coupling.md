Map Name
Substrate–Collapse Coupling
Map Type
System Interaction Map / Deep Structural Coupling
Purpose
This map defines how substrate density and collapse physics interact.
Collapse does not originate from the surface layer — it emerges from density tension, fault line instability, and resonance amplification within the substrate.
Substrate–collapse coupling determines:
• 	where collapse forms
• 	how collapse propagates
• 	what shape collapse takes
• 	how collapse interacts with residue and boundaries
• 	how collapse dissipates
This is a root-level interaction map.

1. Coupling Overview
Collapse is a surface expression of deep substrate tension.
Coupling strength depends on:
• 	density magnitude
• 	gradient steepness
• 	fault line activity
• 	resonance pocket proximity
• 	inversion presence
High coupling = collapse is likely, strong, and structurally coherent.
Low coupling = collapse is weak, diffuse, or fails to form.

2. Coupling Factors
A. Density Magnitude
High substrate density increases collapse susceptibility.
Effects:
• 	collapse fronts form more easily
• 	collapse vectors strengthen
• 	vortex formation becomes more stable
Low density resists collapse formation.

B. Gradient Steepness
Steep gradients create directional collapse behavior.
Effects:
• 	collapse propagates along gradient direction
• 	collapse speed increases
• 	collapse fronts sharpen
Shallow gradients produce slow, diffuse collapse.

C. Fault Line Activity
Fault lines act as collapse conduits.
Effects:
• 	collapse travels along fault lines
• 	collapse intensity increases at intersections
• 	collapse can jump between strata
Fault line intersections are high-risk zones.

D. Resonance Pockets
Resonance pockets amplify collapse behavior.
Effects:
• 	collapse becomes symbolically charged
• 	residue density increases
• 	emotional turbulence intensifies
Resonance pockets create volatile collapse signatures.

E. Density Inversions
Density inversions are the strongest coupling condition.
Effects:
• 	collapse fronts form spontaneously
• 	collapse vectors become unstable
• 	discharge events become likely
• 	residue becomes turbulent
Inversions are rare but extremely dangerous.

3. Collapse Formation Pathways
Collapse forms through three primary pathways:
A. Gradient-Driven Collapse
• 	triggered by steep density gradients
• 	directional
• 	predictable
• 	low symbolic charge
B. Fault Line Collapse
• 	triggered by fault line instability
• 	fast propagation
• 	high structural coherence
• 	medium symbolic charge
C. Inversion Collapse
• 	triggered by density inversion
• 	explosive formation
• 	high symbolic charge
• 	strong discharge likelihood
Inversion collapse is the most volatile.

4. Collapse Propagation Modes
A. Linear Propagation
• 	follows gradient direction
• 	stable and predictable
• 	low turbulence
B. Radial Propagation
• 	expands outward from fault line intersection
• 	medium turbulence
• 	high residue formation
C. Spiral Propagation
• 	forms around vortex points
• 	high turbulence
• 	strong symbolic distortion
Spiral propagation is associated with collapse vortices.

5. Collapse–Residue Interaction
Substrate density determines residue behavior.
High Density
• 	dense residue
• 	slow decay
• 	high symbolic clarity
Medium Density
• 	moderate residue
• 	predictable decay
• 	stable symbolic behavior
Low Density
• 	minimal residue
• 	fast decay
• 	low symbolic clarity
Residue is a density echo.

6. Collapse Dissipation
Collapse dissipates when coupling weakens.
Dissipation triggers:
• 	gradient flattening
• 	fault line stabilization
• 	resonance pocket discharge
• 	inversion resolution
Dissipation is rapid once coupling drops below threshold.

7. Coupling Index
The Substrate–Collapse Coupling Index (SCCI) is derived from:
• 	density magnitude
• 	gradient steepness
• 	fault line activity
• 	resonance pocket proximity
• 	inversion presence
High SCCI = high collapse likelihood.
Low SCCI = collapse-resistant region.
