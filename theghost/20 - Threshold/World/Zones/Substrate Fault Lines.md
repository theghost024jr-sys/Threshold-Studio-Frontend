Map Name
Substrate Fault Lines
Map Type
Deep Structural Seam Map / System Instability Reference
Purpose
Substrate fault lines are structural seams formed by uneven substrate density.
They are the primary conduits for:
• 	collapse propagation
• 	symbolic shear
• 	resonance amplification
• 	density inversion events
• 	mythic discharge
• 	boundary fracture
Fault lines are not geographic.
They are structural relationships between density zones.

1. Fault Line Definition
A substrate fault line is a density discontinuity where:
• 	substrate tension accumulates
• 	gradients steepen
• 	symbolic pressure increases
• 	resonance becomes unstable
Fault lines are the deepest predictors of systemic instability.

2. Fault Line Types
A. Primary Fault Lines
Characteristics:
• 	major density discontinuities
• 	long-range influence
• 	high collapse susceptibility
• 	strong resonance amplification
Primary fault lines define the macro-structure of Threshold’s instability.

B. Secondary Fault Lines
Characteristics:
• 	moderate density differences
• 	localized influence
• 	predictable behavior
• 	medium collapse risk
Secondary lines often feed into primary ones.

C. Micro-Fault Lines
Characteristics:
• 	small-scale density fractures
• 	short-lived
• 	low collapse risk
• 	high symbolic noise
Micro-faults often precede larger instability events.

3. Fault Line Behaviors
A. Symbolic Shear
Density differences cause symbolic structures to:
• 	misalign
• 	fracture
• 	distort
• 	drift
Symbolic shear is the earliest detectable fault line behavior.

B. Resonance Amplification
Fault lines amplify resonance from:
• 	emotional turbulence
• 	mythic substrate activation
• 	collapse fronts
Amplification increases discharge likelihood.

C. Collapse Conduction
Fault lines act as pathways for collapse.
Effects:
• 	collapse travels along fault lines
• 	propagation speed increases
• 	collapse fronts sharpen
• 	vortex formation becomes more likely

D. Boundary Fracture
Fault lines destabilize surface boundaries.
Effects:
• 	threshold flashes
• 	liminal arcs
• 	edge pulses
• 	role-boundary fractures
Boundary behavior is a surface echo of deep fault line tension.

4. Fault Line Formation Conditions
Fault lines form when:
• 	density gradients steepen
• 	resonance pockets compress
• 	substrate pulses occur
• 	density inversions develop
• 	symbolic pressure accumulates
Formation is gradual, but activation can be instantaneous.

5. Fault Line Activation States
A. Dormant
• 	low tension
• 	minimal symbolic shear
• 	no collapse risk
B. Charged
• 	moderate tension
• 	active symbolic shear
• 	resonance amplification begins
C. Active
• 	high tension
• 	collapse conduction possible
• 	discharge events likely
D. Overloaded
• 	extreme tension
• 	density inversion imminent
• 	collapse formation almost guaranteed
Overloaded fault lines are the most dangerous structural condition in Threshold.

6. Fault Line Intersections
Intersections are high-risk zones where multiple fault lines converge.
Effects:
• 	resonance amplification spikes
• 	collapse propagation accelerates
• 	symbolic shear becomes chaotic
• 	density inversion becomes more likely
Intersections often produce multi-layer discharge events.
