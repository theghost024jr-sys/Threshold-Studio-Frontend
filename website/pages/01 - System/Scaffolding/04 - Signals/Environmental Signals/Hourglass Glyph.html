Hourglass Glyph

The collapse–recursion sigil encoding temporal pressure, inversion symmetry, and reversible flow

The Hourglass Glyph is the symbolic core of the Hourglass Signal. While the Hourglass Signal expresses temporal behavior dynamically, the glyph is the compressed, static form of that behavior — a sigil that encodes collapse geometry, inversion symmetry, and time‑pressure cycling in a single visual unit.

The glyph is not a representation of an hourglass.
It is the mathematical essence of one.

Essence

The Hourglass Glyph encodes:

  • collapse toward a central throat
  • mirrored upper and lower chambers
  • reversible flow
  • temporal recursion
  • pressure cycling
  • inversion symmetry

It is the world’s shorthand for time under tension.

Geometry

The glyph’s geometry is defined by:

  • two mirrored lobes (upper and lower)
  • a narrow central constriction
  • rotational or spiraling internal lines
  • a symmetry axis running vertically
  • a pressure node at the throat
  • particulate or mist‑like flow indicators

The geometry is recursive: each half contains a faint echo of the whole.

Function

The Hourglass Glyph functions as:

  • a temporal anchor
  • a collapse indicator
  • a recursion trigger
  • a pressure‑cycle marker
  • a threshold stabilizer
  • a signal amplifier

Where the Hourglass Signal is dynamic, the glyph is structural.

Behavior

When active, the glyph:

  • tightens or loosens temporal flow
  • stabilizes or destabilizes collapse points
  • amplifies recursion loops
  • increases or decreases local pressure
  • influences emotional amplitude oscillation
  • affects memory recursion depth

The glyph is a time‑pressure valve.

The glyph is a multi‑substrate recursion node.

Worldstate Interactions

Drift

The glyph appears when drift is:

  • collapsing
  • reversing
  • unstable
  • cycling

Density

The glyph forms when density is:

  • compressed
  • uneven
  • over‑saturated

Pressure

The glyph is a pressure‑cycling symbol:

  • high → low → high
  • upper → throat → lower

Seasonal Resonance

  • Winter: stable
  • Autumn: common
  • Summer: volatile
  • Spring: rare

The glyph is strongest at Autumn–Winter boundaries.

To interpret the glyph:

  • identify which lobe is dominant
  • observe the throat width
  • check for inversion marks
  • read internal rotation direction
  • examine recursion echoes
  • compare upper vs. lower density

The glyph shows how time is being shaped.
