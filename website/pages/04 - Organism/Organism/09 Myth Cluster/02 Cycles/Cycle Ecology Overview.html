2. THE FIVE ECOLOGICAL STATES OF THE CYCLE

Each phase produces a distinct ecological condition.

A. Coherence Ecology (Circle)

The ecology of gathering and stability

Environmental behavior:

  • inward‑curving paths
  • radial flora growth
  • stable microclimates

Species behavior:

  • clustering
  • cooperative foraging
  • territorial stabilization

Drift behavior:

  • gathering drift
  • low turbulence

Resonance behavior:

  • harmonic, low‑variance hum

Ecological function:
Builds structure and alignment.

B. Tension Ecology (Gate)

The ecology of pressure and over‑definition

Environmental behavior:

  • narrowing corridors
  • vertical growth
  • directional wind funnels

Species behavior:

  • bracing
  • defensive posturing
  • resource hoarding

Drift behavior:

  • linear compression
  • rising density

Resonance behavior:

  • sharp, directional resonance

Ecological function:
Reveals strain and prepares for fracture.

C. Collapse Ecology (Crown)

The ecology of dissolution and scattering

Environmental behavior:

  • path fracturing
  • debris shedding
  • unstable weather

Species behavior:

  • dispersal
  • shedding forms
  • rapid adaptation or retreat

Drift behavior:

  • scatter drift
  • turbulence

Resonance behavior:

  • diffuse, unstable patterns

Ecological function:
Breaks stagnation and redistributes resources.

D. Void Ecology (Tree)

The ecology of stillness and dormancy

Environmental behavior:

  • still air
  • muted sound
  • dormant flora

Species behavior:

  • hibernation
  • metabolic reduction
  • minimal movement

Drift behavior:

  • downward settling
  • low velocity

Resonance behavior:

  • muted, low‑frequency hum

Ecological function:
Restores baseline and absorbs residue.

E. Renewal Ecology (Lantern)

The ecology of ignition and early growth

Environmental behavior:

  • warm pockets
  • early sprouts
  • upward drift spirals

Species behavior:

  • exploratory movement
  • early reproduction
  • re‑forming social structures

Drift behavior:

  • spark drift
  • upward flicker

Resonance behavior:

  • rising, flickering patterns

Ecological function:
Initiates new forms and re‑establishes coherence.

3. ECOLOGICAL DRIVERS OF THE CYCLE

Cycle Ecology is driven by five forces:

1. Pressure

Determines phase direction (inward, vertical, outward, downward, upward).

2. Drift Density

Determines ecological movement and species behavior.

3. Resonance Stability

Determines environmental coherence or volatility.

4. Structural Load

Determines when ecosystems stabilize, strain, or collapse.

5. Metabolic Demand

Determines when ecosystems rest or ignite.

These forces shape ecological transitions.

4. ECOLOGICAL TRANSITIONS — HOW ECOSYSTEMS SHIFT PHASES

Each transition has a distinct ecological signature.

Coherence → Tension

  • over‑crowding
  • resource saturation
  • rigid growth patterns

Tension → Collapse

  • species dispersal
  • micro‑fractures in habitat
  • directional drift breaking

Collapse → Void

  • exhaustion of movement
  • settling debris
  • dormancy onset

Void → Renewal

  • warming soil
  • early sprouts
  • exploratory species behavior

Renewal → Coherence

  • clustering
  • stabilizing patterns
  • harmonic resonance return

Transitions are ecological hinge‑points.

5. ECOLOGICAL FEEDBACK LOOPS

Cycle Ecology is shaped by feedback.

Positive Feedback (amplifying)

  • Coherence strengthens Coherence
  • Tension intensifies Tension
  • Collapse accelerates Collapse

Negative Feedback (balancing)

  • Collapse reduces Tension
  • Void reduces Collapse
  • Renewal reduces Void

Feedback loops determine ecological stability.

6. ECOLOGICAL PATHOLOGIES — WHEN THE CYCLE MISFIRES

1. Saturation Lock (Coherence pathology)

Ecosystem becomes over‑stable → stagnation.

2. Pressure Loop (Tension pathology)

Chronic strain → species stress.

3. Cascading Collapse (Collapse pathology)

Runaway dissolution → habitat fragmentation.

4. Deep Void Saturation (Void pathology)

Prolonged dormancy → ecological stasis.

5. Premature Renewal (Renewal pathology)

Early growth → fragile ecosystems.

Pathologies require intervention or navigation.
