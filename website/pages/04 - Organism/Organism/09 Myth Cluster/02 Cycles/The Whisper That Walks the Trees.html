They say the wind in the trees is not wind at all. Not really. Long before people learned to name forests, there was a messenger who walked the world without feet, without form, without a place to rest. It carried news between distant things — mountain to river, river to valley, valley to sea — but it had no voice of its own. So it borrowed the trees. When it needed to speak, it slipped into their branches and let the leaves do the talking. A rustle for greeting. A shiver for warning. A long, low sigh for sorrow. The trees understood this arrangement. They liked being part of the conversation. But the messenger had one rule: It would only whisper to those who were not listening for answers. If you strained your ears, you’d hear nothing but ordinary wind. If you demanded meaning, the branches stayed still. If you tried to catch the message, it dissolved like breath on glass. But if you walked beneath the canopy with your thoughts loosened — not empty, just unguarded — the whisper might choose you. It never said anything directly. It spoke in fragments, half‑shaped impressions: A memory you didn’t know you’d forgotten. A direction you hadn’t realized you needed. A feeling that arrived like a hand on your shoulder. Some people say the whisper carries news from faraway places. Others say it carries news from faraway versions of yourself. The oldest storytellers say it doesn’t carry news at all. They say it carries permission. Permission to move. Permission to change. Permission to let something go. And that’s why the wind sounds different in every forest. Not because the trees are different — but because the message is.
