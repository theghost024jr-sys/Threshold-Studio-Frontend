A world is a coherent space of laws, relationships, identities, and transformations that can sustain meaning over time. It is not defined by geography or visuals, but by the logic that makes events, entities, and experiences possible. A world is the largest unit of structure in which rules, agency, and change can actually operate.

What makes something a world

A world has several defining properties that distinguish it from a map, a story, or a game level.

1. A world has a substrate

This is the underlying logic that everything rests on. It can be physical (gravity, matter), symbolic (myth, meaning), emotional (stance, resonance), or computational (state machines, rulesets). The substrate determines what is possible and what is impossible.

2. A world has entities with identity

A world contains beings, objects, forces, or concepts that have:

  • properties
  • behaviors
  • relationships
  • persistence

They are not just decorations; they exist within the world’s logic.

3. A world has rules that govern behavior

These rules define:

  • how things move
  • how things interact
  • how things change
  • what consequences follow actions

Rules give the world coherence and make it feel alive rather than arbitrary.

4. A world has space for agency

A world allows an agent—player, character, entity—to act within it. Agency only matters when the environment can respond, resist, or transform based on those actions.

5. A world has continuity and memory

A world persists across time. It can:

  • evolve
  • accumulate history
  • remember states
  • reflect past actions

This continuity is what makes transformation meaningful.

6. A world has revelation

A world is not static; it reveals itself. New layers, meanings, or mechanics become visible as the agent moves, grows, or changes stance. Revelation is what makes a world feel deep rather than flat.

What a world is not

A world is not:

  • a map
  • a setting
  • a story
  • a level
  • a collection of assets
  • a theme

Those are components or expressions of a world, but they are not the world itself.

A world is the logic that makes those components meaningful.

Why worlds matter in design

Worlds allow:

  • emergent behavior
  • systemic storytelling
  • identity formation
  • meaningful choice
  • transformation
  • coherence across complexity

A world is the only structure large enough to hold:

  • laws
  • entities
  • relationships
  • consequences
  • myth
  • agency

Without a world, you can have mechanics or narrative, but not meaningful experience.

Why Threshold qualifies as a world

Threshold has:

  • a substrate (emotional physics, intention, resonance)
  • entities (spirits, animals, biomes, bands)
  • rules (alignment, contribution, traversal)
  • agency (player stance, movement, attention)
  • continuity (worldstate index, evolving layers)
  • revelation (band transitions, mythic layers)

This is the full architecture of a world, not a game or a setting.
