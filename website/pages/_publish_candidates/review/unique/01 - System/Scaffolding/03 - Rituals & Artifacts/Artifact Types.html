Artifact Types

Taxonomy of rooted symbolic constructs and their functional roles

Artifacts are rooted symbolic organs that store, conduct, amplify, or transform symbolic charge. Their behavior depends on their class, resonance signature, and substrate interfaces. This taxonomy defines the six canonical artifact types in Threshold and the ecological roles they play.

Conductive Artifacts

Conductive artifacts move symbolic charge between nodes, rituals, or substrates. They act as channels, bridges, or circuits.

  • Function — transfer charge without altering its orientation.
  • Strength — stabilizes charge flow across unstable regions.
  • Risk — overload if charge exceeds conduction capacity.
  • Examples — bracelets, rods, conduits, ritual pathways.

Conductive artifacts are essential for multi-phase rituals and cross-substrate operations.

Resonant Artifacts

Resonant artifacts amplify or modulate symbolic resonance. They shape frequency, intensity, and harmonic alignment.

  • Function — increase or refine resonance signatures.
  • Strength — enhances ritual activation and symbolic clarity.
  • Risk — resonance inversion under emotional turbulence.
  • Examples — crowns, chimes, harmonic glyphs.

Resonant artifacts are stabilizers in high-drift environments.

Anchoring Artifacts

Anchoring artifacts stabilize symbolic density, drift, or environmental turbulence. They create fixed points in symbolic space.

  • Function — reduce drift and prevent collapse.
  • Strength — creates stable zones for rituals or traversal.
  • Risk — density saturation if overloaded.
  • Examples — altars, stones, rooted glyphs.

Anchors are the backbone of symbolic ecology.

Transformative Artifacts

Transformative artifacts alter symbolic charge orientation or meaning. They are mutation engines.

  • Function — invert, rotate, or reconfigure symbolic charge.
  • Strength — enables controlled mutation and state change.
  • Risk — parasitic binding or uncontrolled drift.
  • Examples — lattices, prisms, catalytic glyphs.

These artifacts are essential for transformation rituals and state transitions.

Memory Artifacts

Memory artifacts store lineage, recursion, or symbolic history. They act as archives, echo chambers, or lineage anchors.

  • Function — preserve symbolic states across time.
  • Strength — stabilizes recursion and prevents drift decay.
  • Risk — echo loops or recursive lock.
  • Examples — ledgers, inscriptions, seed-records.

Memory artifacts are critical for mythic continuity and long-form rituals.

Interface Artifacts

Interface artifacts connect rituals, roles, or substrates. They are cross-layer connectors.

  • Function — create bridges between symbolic, emotional, memory, and scene substrates.
  • Strength — enables multi-substrate operations and hybrid rituals.
  • Risk — substrate bleed or cross-layer corruption.
  • Examples — glyphs, seals, hybrid surfaces.

Interface artifacts are the most volatile but also the most powerful.

Cross-Class Hybrids

Some artifacts express multiple classes simultaneously. These hybrids emerge when:

  • density gradients overlap
  • resonance signatures fuse
  • rooting occurs during symbolic weather
  • a prototype (e.g., First Seed) influences formation

Hybrids are rare and often mythic in scale.

Ecological Roles

Each artifact type plays a distinct role in the symbolic ecology:

  • Conductive — movement
  • Resonant — amplification
  • Anchoring — stabilization
  • Transformative — mutation
  • Memory — preservation
  • Interface — connection

Together, they form the object-side mechanics of symbolic physics.
