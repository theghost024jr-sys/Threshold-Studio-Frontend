Ambiguous is the only choice that keeps the cosmos alive — and in Threshold, ambiguity isn’t vagueness. It’s the world refusing to collapse the waveform.
When two players echo across different Fib Flowers — whether by returning seeds at the same moment, or by hitting the same emotional stance, or by aligning intention across ontologies — the entanglement flash happens, but the cause is never declared.

The world never says:
“You did this.”

It only says:
“Something happened.”

And that ambiguity is what turns the event into myth.

What “ambiguous” actually means in Threshold

The system never reveals which channel triggered the entanglement:

  • Was it the seed‑return synchronicity?
  • Was it two players feeling the same internal stance?
  • Was it two intentions aligning across flowers?
  • Was it coincidence?
  • Was it the world itself reaching out?

Players can’t know.
They can only interpret.

This is the same logic as the shimmer:
the world responds to intention, but never explains itself.

How the ambiguity shows up in the world

When the entanglement flash happens:

  • the void shimmers
  • the petals distort
  • the hub resonates
  • the pulse stretches beyond the flower
  • the interference collapses

But there is no UI, no message, no confirmation.

The world reacts, but it doesn’t narrate.

Players are left with:

  • a feeling
  • a moment
  • a rumor
  • a story

And that’s the point.

Why ambiguity is essential for cross‑flower resonance

Ambiguity preserves:

  • mystery (no one can force it)
  • meaning (players interpret it)
  • social ignition (stories spread)
  • mythic scale (the world is bigger than explanation)
  • emergence (the system stays alive)

If the world ever confirmed the cause, the cosmos would collapse into a mechanic.
Ambiguity keeps it a phenomenon.

How players experience it

Two players in different flowers do something — maybe the same thing, maybe not — and then:

  • a pulse stretches
  • a shimmer appears at the edge
  • a standing wave forms
  • the void lights up
  • the world shudders

And then it’s gone.

Players say:

  • “Did you see that?”
  • “What caused it?”
  • “Was that you?”
  • “Was that another flower?”
  • “Was that the world?”

No one knows.
No one can know.
They can only believe.

Why this is the correct architecture

Threshold is built on:

  • intention
  • resonance
  • alignment
  • meaning
  • emergence

Ambiguity is not a lack of clarity.
It’s the mechanic that allows meaning to form socially, not mechanically.

The world doesn’t tell players what happened.
Players tell each other.
